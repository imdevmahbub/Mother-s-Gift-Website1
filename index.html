How it works

Three gestures. One long, quiet thread.

You send one thing. Behind it, a small team keeps working — quietly, respectfully, and entirely on her terms — for as long as she wants us.

01 — The gift
THE GIFT

Something tangible, chosen with care.

A considered object — linen, ceramic, a scent she'll remember. It arrives beautifully wrapped with your message, and never mentions logistics, forms, or obligation.

02 — The arrival
THE ARRIVAL

Then a person, quietly, on her side.

Within a day, a care coordinator introduces herself. No calls unless she wants them. Meals, hands, sleep, laundry — offered before she thinks to ask.

03 — The after
THE AFTER

Care that adapts as she changes.

Week three looks nothing like week one. What arrives shifts with her recovery, her sleep, her mood — because she tells us, or because she chooses to tell us nothing at all.

The First Week Check

A quiet, practical read for the days after coming home. Written by midwives, edited by mothers, and mercifully short.

In this issue

01 — Sleep

Sleep, honestly.

Nobody sleeps in the first week. The goal isn't eight hours — it's one uninterrupted stretch, however short, and it changes almost everything.

A single four-hour block does more for recovery than six broken ones. If someone offers to take a night feed, the answer is yes — not because you can't, but because the arithmetic is on your side.

The four-hour rule

Protect one four-hour stretch. Anything less than three is a nap, not sleep, and won't reset you.

Practical

Daylight beats caffeine

Ten minutes outside before noon shifts your rhythm more reliably than a second coffee.

Gentle
“You are not failing at sleep. You are recovering from birth while running a night shift.”

02 — Eating

Eating when you can't.

Warm, one-handed, under four minutes. That's the whole brief. Anything that requires a plate and a table is a project right now.

Broth with a spoon of rice

Warm, hydrating, and almost impossible to spill on a baby.

Two minutes

Keep one plate at counter height

A bowl of fruit and nuts at arm's length gets eaten. Anything in a cupboard doesn't.

Set-up

03 — Asking

Asking without apologising.

People want to help and mostly don't know how. Specific requests are kinder than vague ones — to them as much as to you.

  • “Could you take her for an hour at 7am on Thursday?”
  • “Can you bring dinner on Tuesday, in a dish I don't have to return?”
  • “I'd love it if you came and did the washing up and said nothing.”
  • “Not this week — can I come back to you in ten days?”

Declining is not a failure of gratitude. It's information, and people handle it far better than they handle silence.

04 — Visitors

Visitors, gently handled.

One short message sent before anyone arrives prevents ten awkward conversations at the door.

A message you can copy

“We'd love to see you. Half an hour, no gifts, and please don't ring the bell.”

Script

One window, not a weekend

Give one clear window a week. Everyone relaxes when the visit has an end.

Boundaries

05 — Recovery

Your body, week one.

Nothing here is a warning — only a note on what's usual, so you're not surprised by your own body.

Bleeding that gradually lightens, afterpains that sharpen during feeds, night sweats around day four, and a wave of emotion around day five are all ordinary. Anything that feels wrong to you is worth a call, regardless of what a leaflet says.

“Trust the person having the experience. That's you.”

06 — The check-in

The check-in list.

Six questions worth asking yourself, once a day, in the same order. Ten seconds, no writing required.

The list

Have I eaten something warm? Have I had four hours? Have I been outside? Have I said no to one thing? Have I been touched kindly? Have I asked for one thing?

Ten seconds

If two answers are no

Then something should change today, not next week. That's what the circle is for.
